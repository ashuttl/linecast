# Where the numbers come from

linecast draws on many providers, and where a country has its own scale or its own published figure, linecast prefers that to a global model. This page says, for each kind of data, which source is asked first, what stands in when it does not answer, what linecast computes itself, and what that computation was checked against. [Credits](#credits), at the end, has the same in a line each, with the licenses.

Every request goes out with a User-Agent that names linecast and its version. Nothing here needs a key except TideCheck, which is optional.

## Location

The location is the `--location` flag, else `WEATHER_LOCATION`, else the one saved with `linecast location set`, else wherever the network says the machine is. A saved place was resolved to coordinates when it was saved, so reading it back never touches the network.

With nothing saved or given, the machine's address is looked up at [ipinfo.io](https://ipinfo.io/), then [ipwho.is](https://ipwho.is/), then [GeoJS](https://www.geojs.io/), each given three seconds, none needing a key. Only the coordinates and the country are taken. The answer is cached for an hour, so a laptop that moves is found again, and when all three fail the command says it could not determine the location. The accuracy is whatever the service's database has for the address, which is usually the city and sometimes the wrong one; that is why the README suggests saving a location.

A place name goes to [Open-Meteo's geocoder](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/geocoding-api), asked in the display language, ten results, and the first is taken; there is no disambiguation beyond the geocoder's own ranking, and `linecast location search` lists the rest. A regional variant asks in its base language, since the geocoder takes a language alone, and Traditional Chinese asks for Taiwan's, the tag its index knows. [Photon](https://photon.komoot.io/), on OpenStreetMap's data, stands in when Open-Meteo does not answer; it can name places in English, German, and French only, and is asked in English otherwise. When neither answers the command stops and says the search failed. The label is the name, the region, and the country, as the geocoder gave them.

Coordinates become a name through [Nominatim](https://nominatim.org/), and only Nominatim, at its town-level zoom, held to one request a second as its policy asks, with each answer cached for a day. It is asked twice when it needs to be: once with no language, for the country and the address in the country's own language, which is what the alert feeds name their areas in; and once in the display language, for the header, where a regional variant asks for its own names first (fr-CA, then fr) and Traditional Chinese asks for the script and the regions that write it. A place you typed keeps the label the geocoder gave it, since that names what you asked for, where a reverse lookup names whichever boundary encloses the point. When no name comes at all the header shows the coordinates to two decimals, never the time zone's city, which can be a continent away.

The country drives the defaults and the national sources. For your own defaults, the units, the clock, and the first day of the week, it is the saved location's country or the IP lookup's, read from the cache and never fetched; only the United States defaults to imperial. For the place shown, which decides the alert feed, the air quality scale, the tide service, the prayer convention, and the Hebrew calendar's diaspora days, it is Nominatim's country for the point. Hong Kong and Macau are read from the second-level code Nominatim gives, since its country for both is China. The time zone comes from Open-Meteo: the weather forecast carries it, and the other views ask the same endpoint for the zone name alone and keep it for a month. A machine located by its address uses its own clock.

**Checked against:** nothing outside the services themselves; a location is what they say it is. A scheduled suite asks each of them live: the three address lookups must answer with a country, Open-Meteo and Photon must put Westbrook, Maine within its degree of latitude and longitude, and Nominatim must return Portland, Maine as the United States. The search tests read real Photon and Nominatim answers saved as fixtures.

## Weather

The forecast is [Open-Meteo's](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs), which blends the national and global models and picks the best for the place; linecast names no model, so the choice is Open-Meteo's. One request brings the current conditions, seven days ahead and one behind by the hour (temperature, felt temperature, precipitation and its probability, snowfall, wind, gusts and direction, weather code, humidity, dew point, UV, and cloud cover), and by the day the highs and lows, precipitation, wind, and sunrise and sunset. Open-Meteo converts the units, snowfall included: centimetres, or inches when the rain is in inches. The forecast is cached for an hour, and a cached forecast from an earlier day is refetched however young it is. When the service cannot be reached the cache stands in whatever its age, and an amber line names the day the forecast was made and says a newer one could not be fetched, and `--json` sets `stale`; with no cache at all the command stops and says so.

The header, the prose, and the one-line summary read Open-Meteo's current block as it is: nothing there is computed, and a value the service leaves out is left off the line. The felt temperature is Open-Meteo's. The sentence that says whether humidity, wind, or the sun is behind it inverts the Australian Bureau of Meteorology's apparent-temperature formula, and the comment beside it says the two terms were checked against the API hour by hour and match to within a few tenths overnight. Sunrise and sunset in the weather view are Open-Meteo's daily values. Open-Meteo stamps a whole response with one UTC offset, the one in force when it was asked, so on a clock-change day linecast reads the times back through the zone and the day has its 23 or 25 hours.

The line on how today compares with a normal day, and the typical-year scale the chart defaults to, come from [Open-Meteo's archive](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api): the daily high, low, and precipitation for the last ten complete calendar years, one download per year, cached for a week and keyed so that it rolls on the first of January. The archive is asked without a model name, so which reanalysis answers is Open-Meteo's choice too. A normal day is the plain mean of that calendar day across the ten years, with no window around it, so the 29th of February has only the two or three leap years. The comparison is today's forecast high against that mean high; within 1.5 °C, or 2.5 °F, it reads as near average. The typical year's range is the mean of each year's hottest high and coldest low, widened to take in the forecast on screen.

**Checked against:** nothing outside Open-Meteo; the forecast and the archive are its numbers, shown as sent. The tests run the renderer on a real forecast response for Toronto saved as a fixture, and the scheduled live suite asks for Portland, Maine and expects a week of hourly rows and seven sunrises.

## Air quality

The pollutant concentrations come from [Open-Meteo's air quality API](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/air-quality-api), which serves the Copernicus CAMS global model at about 40 km, and CAMS Europe at 10 km over Europe. Open-Meteo also computes the US EPA AQI and the European index from them, and the header shows the US number by default, colored on its scale. The hourly series of PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide, and ozone for the past day come with it, so that a country's own index can be computed on the device.

Because the source is a model, not a monitor, a number can differ from the one on a local station's page, and a plume over one town can be missed. Where a country publishes the index it reports, linecast reads that first.

### India

In India the header shows the CPCB's National AQI (2014) instead of the US number, with the category the CPCB's bulletins print: Good, Satisfactory, Moderate, Poor, Very Poor, Severe. It is computed from the pollutants: each is averaged over its window (24 hours for the particulates, nitrogen dioxide, and sulphur dioxide; 8 hours for ozone and carbon monoxide), mapped onto the shared 0 to 500 index through its own breakpoints, and the index is the worst sub-index. As the CPCB does, linecast reports no index without particulate data, and does not average a window more than half empty. The colors follow the CPCB's wider bands.

### Canada

In Canada the header shows the Air Quality Health Index, on Health Canada's scale of 1 to 10+ with its risk level: Low risk to 3, Moderate to 6, High to 10, and Very high past that. The label reads AQHI in English and CAS, cote air santé, in French, with the risk level in French for a French reader.

The number is Environment Canada's own wherever it has one, read from the [GeoMet API](https://api.weather.gc.ca/):

- The **observations** feed carries the hourly AQHI for about 120 communities, as each province reports it. A place within 75 km of a community with a reading from the last two hours takes that reading. Quebec runs its own index and reports no observations here.
- Otherwise the **forecasts** feed, which carries hourly forecasts for some 200 communities, Montréal among them; the latest issue's value for the hour nearest now stands in.
- Beyond the reach of either, the index is **computed** from the pollutants. Health Canada's formula takes the three-hour trailing means of nitrogen dioxide and ozone in parts per billion and PM2.5 in µg/m³, sums an exponential term for each, and scales the sum so that 10 sits at the top of the range studied; the result is rounded and never below 1. Open-Meteo states the gases in µg/m³, and they are converted at 25 °C and one atmosphere. British Columbia's AQHI-Plus (2018), since taken up by the other provinces, publishes the greater of that and the current hour's PM2.5 divided by ten and rounded up, so that a smoke plume the slow means would understate is reported at once; linecast applies it everywhere in Canada.

The JSON output says which of the three the number came from.

**Checked against:** on 20 September 2026, a day of clean air, the computed index was compared with Environment Canada's reported value at 70 stations across the country for the same hour. It matched the rounded figure at 36 stations and was within one point at 59, running about half a point high on average, with the model's ozone the main cause. It missed a local smoke reading at Prince George, reported at 4 against a computed 1.6. That gap is why the reported value comes first and the computation is the fallback. The formula, its constants, and the categories are Health Canada's as [summarized on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_Quality_Health_Index_(Canada)); the AQHI-Plus rule is from the [Fraser Basin Council's 2018 report](https://www.fraserbasin.bc.ca/_Library/TR_KAQR/aqhi-aqhi_plus_june_25_2018.pdf) and [British Columbia's AQHI page](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/environment/air-land-water/air/air-quality/aqhi).

## Alerts

The country of the place shown, as Nominatim gives it, decides the feed. One feed serves each country, and there is no second one behind it: when a feed cannot be reached the last copy fetched stands in, whatever its age, with the alerts that have since expired dropped, and a muted line under them says when that copy is from. Where there is no copy the same place says the alerts could not be checked, so a feed that is down does not read as a quiet day. `--json` carries this beside the list as `alerts_status`: `status` is `ok`, `stale`, `unavailable`, or `unsupported`, and `fetched_at` is when the feed last answered, in UTC, or null. A country with no feed shows none, no line about it, and no credit line; Iran is one, since its meteorological organization (IRIMO) publishes no warning feed that linecast has found. Ten countries have a feed of their own and thirty-five read MeteoAlarm, forty-five in all.

Every feed is cached for a quarter of an hour, Hong Kong's for ten minutes. What comes back is sorted from Extreme to Minor and cut to eight. linecast does not translate the text of an alert; the words around it are in the display language, and the alert is in whatever the feed sent. Pressing `o` on an alert opens the feed's own page for it where the feed gives one; the US, Norway, MeteoAlarm, and New Zealand do, Japan, Hong Kong, China, and India get their service's warnings page, and Canada, Germany, and Ireland give no page to open.

- **United States**: the [National Weather Service's](https://www.weather.gov/documentation/services-web-api) alerts for the point, in English. Only alerts whose status is Actual are shown; the feed carries test messages, and one is in the fixtures.
- **Canada**: [Environment Canada's](https://api.weather.gc.ca/) alerts in a box half a degree either side of the point, about 50 km. The feed is in English and French; a French reader gets the French fields and the English where they are missing, and the reverse.
- **Germany**: the DWD's warnings for the point through [Bright Sky](https://brightsky.dev/), in German for a German reader and English otherwise, each falling back to the other.
- **Norway**: [MET Norway's](https://api.met.no/weatherapi/metalerts/2.0/documentation) alerts for the point, as the feed gives them.
- **Ireland**: [Met Éireann's](https://www.met.ie/) warnings for the whole country. A national warning lists the counties it is for, by code, and is shown only in those; the county is the one in the reverse-geocoded address. Marine and environmental warnings list no counties and are shown everywhere, as is any warning when the county is not known or one of its codes is not in linecast's table.
- **Japan**: the [JMA's](https://www.jma.go.jp/bosai/warning/) warning file for the nearest of its 58 forecast offices in the prefecture of the reverse-geocoded address, or the nearest outright when the address names none. The file lists warnings by area, down to the municipality. When the address names a municipality the file lists, its warnings are shown, and the headline is cut to the sentences for it; otherwise every warning in force in the prefecture's areas is shown, or in the whole file if none of its areas carry the prefecture's code. The feed is in Japanese; the event names are linecast's own table, in Japanese for a Japanese reader and English otherwise, with the JMA's headline as issued.
- **Hong Kong**: the [Observatory's](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/wxinfo/dailywx/warnsum.htm) warning summary for the territory, in English, simplified Chinese, or traditional Chinese by the display language.
- **China**: the [CMA's](http://www.nmc.cn/) alarm list for the country, kept when an alert's code names one of the three nearest of the 31 provinces, so the match is to the province. A Chinese reader gets the title as issued; otherwise an English name is put together from the type and the colour.
- **India**: [SACHET](https://sachet.ndma.gov.in/), the national aggregator, one list with a centroid and an area for each alert; an alert is kept when the point is within the disc of that area plus 25 km. Each kept alert's CAP file is then read for the block in the display language, Hindi, Telugu, or another Indian language where the issuer wrote one, else English.
- **New Zealand**: [MetService's](https://alerts.metservice.com/) CAP feed, CC BY 4.0; the point is tested against each alert's polygon, and an alert without one is nationwide.
- **MeteoAlarm**, for Austria, Belgium, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Cyprus, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Israel, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Moldova, Montenegro, the Netherlands, North Macedonia, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Serbia, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Ukraine, and the United Kingdom: the [country's feed](https://feeds.meteoalarm.org/), the whole country at once. Germany, Norway, and Ireland are on MeteoAlarm too, but their own feeds say more. The block in the display language is read, else the English one. Minor warnings are dropped. A warning is placed in three ways, the first that applies: by the polygon in the alert, when there is one; else by the region codes it carries, the EMMA IDs MeteoAlarm defines, the NUTS codes France, Hungary, Bulgaria, Romania, Belgium, and Croatia file by, and the ORP codes Czechia does, looked up in geometry linecast carries; else by reading the area's description against the words of the address Nominatim gave for the point, in the country's own language. An Extreme warning is shown regardless. Switzerland, Estonia, the United Kingdom, Israel, Luxembourg, Sweden, and Ukraine have no vendored regions and rely on the polygons and the text.

The region geometry is MeteoAlarm's geocodes of 31 July 2026 (© EUMETNET, CC BY 4.0), the 2013 NUTS boundaries from [Eurostat GISCO](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/gisco) (© EuroGeographics), which are still the codes France, Hungary, and Croatia file by, and Czechia's 206 ORP from [ČÚZK RÚIAN](https://www.cuzk.gov.cz/) with the [Czech Statistical Office's](https://csu.gov.cz/) code list, simplified to about two hundred metres. The build script has a `check` mode that pulls every live feed and lists the areas it cannot place.

**Checked against:** real responses from every feed, saved as fixtures on named days: an empty New York and a tsunami test message beside a heat advisory for the NWS, a freezing rain warning from Environment Canada, DWD frost, a MET Norway week in March, a Met Éireann gale, Drenthe's MeteoAlarm warnings, a Tokyo warning file, Hong Kong's Black Rainstorm of 25 August 2026, a Shanxi dense-fog yellow, Delhi thunderstorms from SACHET, and MetService's Desert Road snowfall and a Dunedin warning of 31 August 2026, which the tests place inside and outside. The vendored regions are checked by looking up Warsaw, Chodzież, Vienna, Paris, Cayenne, Budapest, Sofia, Bucharest, Antwerp, Skopje, Prague, and Brno and expecting each's code, and a point in the Atlantic expecting none. No test compares a placed alert against a dated real one beyond these.

## Sunshine and the Moon

Everything here is computed on the device from two sets of equations. The `sunshine` day and year views use the NOAA Solar Calculator's simplified equations, with Spencer's three-term equation of time and the declination taken once a day at noon UTC; the comment beside them says about a minute for sunrise and sunset and a third of a degree for the Sun's elevation. Everything else, the moon, the sky, the traditional hours, and the calendars, uses Paul Schlyter's [low-precision ephemeris](https://stjarnhimlen.se/comp/ppcomp.html), itself a simplification of Meeus: the Sun to about a hundredth of a degree, the Moon to a couple of arcminutes from twelve longitude terms and five in latitude, with the Moon's distance left at its two-body value, within a percent. Both use the mean obliquity and mean sidereal time; there is no nutation, no aberration, no ΔT, and no observer height.

Sunrise and sunset are the Sun's upper limb, refracted, at 0.833° below the horizon; the sunshine view solves the hour angle in closed form, and the ephemeris bisects to the second. Where the hour angle has no solution the day is polar and reads as midnight sun or polar night. The day length's change from yesterday holds the same UTC offset for both days, so a clock change does not show as an hour. Moonrise and moonset are found by stepping the day in ten-minute steps and bisecting to half a minute, at 0.125° above the geometric horizon, which is refraction and the Moon's parallax partly cancelling. Twilights are the usual 6°, 12°, and 18°. The traditional hours and the calendars have their own pages: [hours.md](hours.md) and [calendars.md](calendars.md).

The phase is the Moon's ecliptic longitude past the Sun's, and the lit fraction follows from the true angle between them. A phase is named new, quarter, or full within a sixtieth of a cycle, about twelve hours, of the instant, as the printed almanacs label the day. The instants of the principal phases are found by search and bisection, the equinoxes and solstices by Meeus's series (chapter 27), valid from 1000 to 3000 and good to a minute or so with TT taken as UTC, and the full moons are named as the Old Farmer's Almanac names them, the Harvest Moon nearest the September equinox, the Hunter's Moon after it, and a Blue Moon the second in a month.

The disc is lit as a sphere. The bright limb's angle is Meeus's (equation 48.5); the Moon's axis is taken as the ecliptic pole, which leaves out the degree and a half of libration; both are turned into screen bearings by the parallactic angle, so the shadow leans as it does over your horizon. The earthshine is set at a fifth of full light, scaled by how much of the disc is dark, so it is gone at full moon, and the halo is decoration. The face is NASA SVS's [CGI Moon Kit](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4720), the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter's colour mosaic at 1k, reduced to a 512 by 256 grayscale with the near side's highlands as the white point, and drawn from the mean sub-Earth point, so the libration of the month does not show; the far side is seen only by turning the disc. The stars behind the Moon are the Yale Bright Star Catalogue to magnitude 6.5, the real ones for the moment, each in its true direction from the Moon, with the Moon turned back into the catalogue's J2000 frame for the comparison; but the disc is drawn far larger than scale and the field is a fisheye with the screen's corner about 90° from the Moon, so the distances are not true, and the field is thinned to a fixed density.

**Checked against:** the eight principal phases of January to April 2026, within a quarter of an hour of the published instants; the Moon's position at four moments of 2026 against PyEphem's ELP2000, within a tenth of a degree, and its bright limb's bearing at four places within 3°; the equinoxes and solstices of 2000 and 2024, within a quarter of an hour of the almanac; and the full-moon names of 2020, 2023, and 2026. Through the hours, the ephemeris's sunrise and sunset are held to Hebcal's within half a minute at four places on four dates of 2026 ([hours.md](hours.md)); the sunshine view's own rise and set, by the NOAA equations, are not in the tests, and on 20 September 2026 they were compared with the ephemeris's at six places on the same four dates and agreed within two minutes, most within one. Moonrise and moonset are checked for shape, not against a table. Property sweeps hold rise before noon before set at every latitude, lunations between 29.2 and 29.9 days for two years, and the lit fraction at full and new.

## The sky

The sky view touches the network for the location and its name and for nothing else. The stars, the figures, the names, the Milky Way, and the deep-sky objects are bundled; the Sun, the Moon, and the planets are computed. [STARS.md](../src/linecast/data/STARS.md) says where each data file is from and how it was built, and [cultures.md](cultures.md) credits the twenty-two other skies.

The stars are the Yale Bright Star Catalogue to magnitude 6.5, 8,404 of them, with 108,520 fainter ones from HYG to magnitude 12 revealed as you zoom: 116,924 in all, the README's "nearly 117,000". Their positions are J2000 in the file and are turned to the equinox of the date when drawn, by the IAU 1976 precession angles (Meeus, chapter 21), one rotation per frame; the Sun, Moon, and planets are computed for that equinox already, so a planet among the stars sits where it should. In 2026 the rotation is about a third of a degree along the ecliptic. The stars are not moved by proper motion or corrected for refraction or aberration. Sidereal time is Meeus's mean value (equation 12.4). The Moon's altitude is lowered by its parallax for the observer, so it sits where it does over your horizon; its azimuth, and the planets, are geocentric.

The planets from Mercury to Neptune follow Schlyter's elements and perturbations, seven terms for Jupiter, seven for Saturn, and three for Uranus, and none for the others, good to a couple of arcminutes for the inner planets and Jupiter and a few for Saturn and beyond; there is no light-time correction. The magnitudes are his formulas, with Saturn's rings tilted by their pole. The Sun is the ephemeris's.

When a star comes out is a limiting magnitude by the Sun's altitude, a hand-set table running from the whole catalogue at 18° down to Venus alone by day, less an atmospheric extinction that grows with air mass toward the horizon. Zooming below 60° adds up to five magnitudes, as optics would. The Milky Way is the diffuse layer of NASA SVS's [Deep Star Maps 2020](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4851), the unresolved starlight from Gaia, reduced to a 1080 by 540 byte raster, and it fades in as the Sun goes from 12° to 18° down. The 107 Messier objects are Olaf Frohn's d3-celestial data, drawn as glows sized to their catalogued extent, schematic rather than photographic. The fifteen asterisms the search offers are a table in the code, each a list of star designations, with no source cited.

The names in each language are baked from three sources in order: Olaf Frohn's star and constellation names for French, Spanish, German, Italian, Finnish, Japanese, Korean, and Chinese; then [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/)'s labels (CC0), the stars matched by their HD number and the constellations by their English name, in twenty-six languages, with a name kept only when it is a proper name and not a designation; then a table of overrides reviewed against each language's Wikipedia. Traditional Chinese is converted character by character from the simplified. Persian takes the title of each fa.wikipedia article, so some stars have their traditional Persian or Arabic names (سهیل، قلب‌العقرب) and others the article's (شباهنگ for Sirius); Wikidata's query service drops the zero-width non-joiner from Persian labels, so those names are overrides. At run time a language's name falls to its base language's, then to the IAU's, and a constellation's to the Latin. Swahili keeps the IAU names but for two sourced ones, noted in STARS.md.

**Checked against:** the precession of Sirius and Polaris to 2026 against PyEphem, and of Meeus's worked example, θ Persei to 2028, within a hundredth of an arcsecond; Mars, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune at their 2025 oppositions and Venus at its 2025 elongation, within a few arcminutes and a few tenths of a magnitude of the published values; the Moon as above; the Milky Way raster bright toward the galactic centre, dark at the poles, and darker in the Great Rift than in the Cygnus cloud; M31's position and size; and that the Moon in the view sits below its geocentric altitude by its parallax. Sidereal time and the stars' altitudes are checked for geometry, the pole at the latitude and the meridian due south, not against an almanac.

## Tides

The station is chosen by the country of the place shown. Canada asks the [Canadian Hydrographic Service](https://www.tides.gc.ca/) first, Queensland the [state's open data](https://www.data.qld.gov.au/), and Hong Kong the [Observatory](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/tide/); then every place asks [NOAA CO-OPS](https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/), which may have a station in reach where the national service does not, at Victoria, British Columbia, for one; then [TideCheck](https://tidecheck.com/) if a key is set; then [Open-Meteo's marine model](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/marine-weather-api) everywhere. The first with a station within 100 nautical miles wins, and the pick is cached for an hour and kept, stale, when the station list cannot be fetched; a list that fails with no stale pick falls through to the next provider. Once a station is chosen there is no falling through: a fetch that fails shows the last copy, and with none the command stops. With no station and no model coverage, inland, it says so.

- **NOAA**: the station list is fetched and kept for a month. Only reference stations are picked by distance; subordinate stations publish highs and lows alone, and are reached by name, with a cosine curve drawn between the extremes. Predictions are six-minute heights above MLLW in the station's own time, a month per request, cached for a day, with NOAA's own highs and lows.
- **Canadian Hydrographic Service**: the station list from the IWLS API, kept for a month, closed stations left out. Five-minute heights and the turning points, in UTC and metres, converted to the station's zone; the turning points come unlabelled, and linecast tells the highs from the lows by their neighbours.
- **Queensland**: the state's CKAN datastore, ten-minute heights above LAT in Queensland time. The packages carry no coordinates, so linecast bundles 69 gauges' positions from their description files or from Nominatim; ten beacon and reef gauges have none and are reached by name. The highs and lows are found from the series.
- **Hong Kong**: thirteen stations with positions from the Observatory's geodata, leaving out two whose predictions are computed rather than observed. Hourly heights above chart datum and the day's highs and lows, in Hong Kong time.
- **TideCheck**: the nearest station within 185 km, by its own search, and its highs and lows for the next 30 days in metres above MLLW, with the curve drawn between them as for a NOAA subordinate station. Every request is counted in a daily tally, which the `--nearby` listing and `doctor` report against the free tier's fifty. At fifty linecast stops asking until the day turns, in UTC, and serves what it has cached; `LINECAST_TIDECHECK_PAID` lifts the cap. The tally is this machine's alone, so another machine on the same key is not counted.
- **Open-Meteo**: hourly sea level above mean sea level from the marine API, snapped to the nearest sea cell, 31 days back and 8 ahead, cached for three hours. There is no station; the place itself is the station. Highs and lows are found from the hourly series with a parabola through each extremum. Whether the series includes surge is not stated by the code and should be taken from Open-Meteo's own documentation.

Nothing is converted between datums; each provider's is passed through, and the footer names the provider. Heights are converted to feet where the units are imperial. The level now, the hover, and the JSON series are linear between samples. The moon's phase in the header and the moonrise and moonset marks on the day row are the ephemeris's, at the station. Wave heights and periods beside the chart are Open-Meteo's marine forecast at the station, and a station with none, up a river, shows no wave line.

**Checked against:** real responses saved as fixtures: NOAA's six-minute predictions and highs and lows for 5 March 2026 and Bar Harbor's metadata; a Queensland datastore page of 26 August 2026; Cheung Chau's hourly heights for three days of August 2026 and the turn of the year, with the first high on 24 August at 05:38 and the alternation of highs and lows across New Year. The Queensland gauge datasets were verified against the monitoring feed's prediction column to within a centimetre on an overlapping day when they were added. The parabolic refinement of Open-Meteo's extremes is held within a quarter of an hour and two hundredths of the height on a synthetic tide. Open-Meteo's model has not been compared with a NOAA station, and no synthesized curve has been compared with a provider's own.

## Radar

[LibreWXR](https://librewxr.net/) is asked first everywhere, from its public index. In the lower 48 states the fallback is NEXRAD from the [Iowa Environmental Mesonet](https://mesonet.agron.iastate.edu/), and elsewhere it is [RainViewer](https://www.rainviewer.com/), then IEM, which returns nothing abroad. The first source whose index answers with frames is used, and a source whose frame comes back short of tiles is stepped down the chain once in a session. `--source` pins one and it is never demoted; pinning IEM abroad draws nothing.

LibreWXR composites real radar where it is published openly and fills the rest of the world with a precipitation model. Its index does not say which is which, so linecast carries seven boxes drawn from LibreWXR's source list, the United States and Canada, Europe, Japan, Taiwan, Malaysia with Singapore and Brunei, the Philippines, and El Salvador, and the footer says "no radar here" outside them. The frames are what the index publishes, about two hours of past frames and an hour of nowcast, at up to zoom 12. RainViewer's free tier serves its Universal Blue colours alone and publishes at zoom 7; IEM is the n0q base reflectivity composite, drawn by its WMS at the exact size of the screen, five minutes a frame for the last three hours, with no forecast. The index is cached for two minutes and served stale on error, past tiles are kept, nowcast tiles for ten minutes, and tiles older than a day are pruned when `radar` starts.

Everything is decoded to dBZ. LibreWXR's raw scheme encodes dBZ in the gray byte with a bit for snow; RainViewer's Universal Blue is inverted through a vendored copy of its published table; IEM's server-coloured image is drawn as sent. Echoes under 10 dBZ are cleared. The `terminal` palette is the dusk ladder passed through the terminal's own colours, keeping the lightness and taking the hue, and `dusk` was measured from LibreWXR's dark-sky tiles; `marangai` follows MetService New Zealand's radar, matched by eye. The satellite layer is LibreWXR's hourly infrared cloud mosaic, at about 8 km, reaching the 72nd parallels. The temperature and wind layers are Open-Meteo's forecast on a lattice of sixty points over the view, snapped to quarter degrees, interpolated between them, with the wind kept as components so the direction does not wrap. The warning polygons are IEM's storm-based warnings for the frame's instant: tornado, severe thunderstorm, flash flood, special marine, and snow squall warnings, with an emergency in magenta; they are drawn on past frames only and only over the United States.

The basemap is Natural Earth: land and lakes at 1:10m (land from a square kilometre, lakes from forty), borders at 1:50m, and populated places above 40,000 or a capital with their names in each language Natural Earth has; the coastline is the land's outline, so fill and stroke agree. It is simplified to about a kilometre and bundled for the whole world.

**Checked against:** the tests run on hand-built indexes and synthetic tiles, with no saved response from any radar service. The Universal Blue decoding is checked against RainViewer's published colour for 20 dBZ and round-trips every visible entry of the table. The basemap's data is checked by looking up the Gulf of Maine, the Great Lakes, and the Caspian. No decoded value has been compared with a radar product outside the app.

## Maps

Terrain is the [AWS Open Data Terrain Tiles](https://registry.opendata.aws/terrain-tiles/) in Mapzen's terrarium encoding, keyless and immutable, so a tile once fetched is kept until the cache is trimmed to its size, 256 MB by default, when `maps` starts. No keyless mirror of them exists, so there is no second source; `LINECAST_ELEVATION_URL` names another. The land is SRTM, GMTED, and the national models the tiles composite, to zoom 13, where SRTM's 30 m grid runs out. The sea floor is ETOPO1, which the tiles carry only to zoom 10, so a tighter view keeps its sea from the zoom-10 grid, and at street zooms from OpenStreetMap's ocean polygon. The coastline is the sea-level contour of the elevation itself, never a second dataset. The relief is lit by a northwest light at 45° with two fainter ones either side, and the exaggeration grows with the ground a pixel covers. The height picks the colour band and the climate picks the ramp, humid, semi-arid, arid, or polar, from the [Köppen-Geiger grid](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.21789074) of Beck et al. (2023) for 1991 to 2020 at a tenth of a degree, CC BY 4.0, bundled whole with the thirty classes folded into those four families and the sea filled from the nearest land so a shoreline never misses. Built-up ground is the [Global Human Settlement Layer's](https://human-settlement.emergency.copernicus.eu/) built-up surface for 2020 (GHSL © European Commission JRC, CC BY 4.0), tiled at three arc-seconds into a pyramid to zoom 9 that linecast publishes from its own bucket; the layer knows the town whose streets nobody has mapped, and an empty `LINECAST_BUILTUP_URL` turns it off.

Streets, water, parks, land cover, and buildings are [OpenFreeMap's](https://openfreemap.org/) planet vector tiles in the OpenMapTiles schema (© OpenMapTiles © OpenStreetMap contributors), found through its TileJSON because the planet is rebuilt weekly under a dated path. The [OpenStreetMap US Tileservice](https://tiles.openstreetmap.us/) (Tiles by OSM US), the same schema rebuilt daily, stands in when OpenFreeMap does not answer, and yesterday's copy of either comes before switching. `LINECAST_VECTOR_TILES_URL` stands alone, with no fallback. linecast decodes the tiles with its own reader. A tile never expires, since its URL is versioned. A name is the tile's `name:` field for the display language, then its base language, then the Latin transliteration, then the local name; nothing is translated by linecast. The terrain view takes its lakes and rivers from the same tiles, since an elevation model gives a lake its surface height, and its borders from Natural Earth, from the radar's basemap; the globe does the same.

The globe is a whole-world mosaic of the same terrain tiles at zooms 1 and 2, baked in, with zoom 3 stitched on the machine for a very tall terminal. The night lights are NASA's [Black Marble 2016](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/earth-at-night/maps/) grayscale map, from Suomi NPP VIIRS, bundled as a seven-level pyramid running from 2048 by 1024 down to 32 by 16, area-averaged at every level so a bright city never fills a coarse pixel with its peak. The ground a display pixel covers picks the level, corrected for the globe's limb and its converging meridians, and the lights fade out as the view closes in and the terrain takes over. It is a historical composite, not live lighting and not a map of outages, and nothing is fetched; [NIGHT_LIGHTS.md](https://github.com/ashuttl/linecast/blob/main/src/linecast/data/NIGHT_LIGHTS.md) has the credits and the format. The terminator is the subsolar point from the sunshine view's declination and equation of time, with daylight ramping in from 9° below the horizon to 3° above. The clouds are [LibreWXR's](https://librewxr.net/) infrared mosaic (CC BY 4.0), the same frames as the radar's satellite layer, the newest taken, at about 8 km, an hour or two behind the clock, reaching the 72nd parallels. Poleward of that nothing sees, and the cap is fractal noise developed against the mosaic's last good ring, cloudy where it is cloudy and clear where it is clear: invented weather, deliberately, so the picture has no hole in it.

Search asks [Photon](https://photon.komoot.io/) while you type, biased toward the view, in English, German, or French where the display language is one of those, and [Nominatim](https://nominatim.org/) when you press Enter and Photon found nothing, in the display language, one request a second, each answer kept for a week. The first result is taken. Directions are [OSRM](http://project-osrm.org/) at [FOSSGIS](https://routing.openstreetmap.de/) for car, bicycle, and foot (© OpenStreetMap contributors), with the project's own demo server standing in for the car alone, one request a second. The steps are the road name, the distance, and a glyph for the manoeuvre; OSRM sends no instruction text and linecast writes none, so only the panel's frame is in the display language.

**Checked against:** the terrarium decoding round-trips Everest's 8,848 m, sea level, and a depth; a real Photon search, a real Nominatim search, and a real route from FOSSGIS are saved as fixtures; the subsolar point is checked at the equinoxes and solstices; and the globe's sampler is checked against a plain implementation. No fetched elevation, tile, or route is compared with a reference outside the app.
